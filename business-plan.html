The core insight

Matchmaking and transport aren't two businesses. They're two ends of one service.

The real pain for collectors isn't finding a car, and it isn't shipping a car. It's that the whole arc — find, vet, negotiate, move, deliver — is fragmented. No one owns it for you.

Front of house

Matchmaking

Intake interview. Search the market and the network. Curate 3–5 real candidates. Coordinate pre-purchase inspections. Negotiate or bid on the client's behalf.

Back of house

White-glove transport

Partner carriers, not owned trucks. Enclosed-first. Condition documentation. Obsessive communication in transit. A thoughtful handoff at delivery — keys, docs, first drive.

The service arc

One engagement. Five steps. One person in front of you the whole way.

Every step is timeboxed. The total calendar flexes with the car and the market — but you always know where you are, what's next, and how long it should take.

01

Intake Listen

30 – 90 minutes · scheduled around you

A structured conversation about the dream: era, marque, originality tolerance, budget, and how the car will live — daily driver, weekend, show, keep.

02

Curate Find

1 – 2 weeks · the best fit, swiftly

BaT, Hemmings, auctions, marque shops, and a collector network built car by car over two decades. You get three to five real candidates — each with a point of view on tradeoffs, not a dump of listings. Rarer specs take longer; we tell you on day one.

03

Vet Verify

Within a week · in person where it counts

Within 200 miles of Greenwich, CT, Tim inspects the car personally. Beyond that radius, remote vetting with a trusted marque specialist — or an in-person visit by arrangement, priced transparently by distance. Either way: docs, history, provenance, and an honest recommendation, not a sales pitch.

04

Negotiate Advocate

24 – 48 hours · decisive, on your side

Offer or auction bid on your behalf. Terms, deposits, paperwork, payment coordination. Nobody disappears after the deposit.

05

Deliver Hand off

3 – 7 days · routed by distance

Enclosed transport via a vetted partner. Photo walkaround before and after. A handoff that feels like a friend dropping off a car, not a freight call.

Full concierge, or any piece of it. Priced clearly either way.

Two paths in. End-to-end flat-fee engagements for buyers who want the whole arc handled. À la carte pricing for clients who only need a step or two. Both are scoped on the intake call and priced transparently — nothing starts until the number is on paper.

How the book grows · referrals

Reputation travels through satisfied clients. The program should too.

Tim's view: the best marketing for a practice like this isn't advertising, it's a past client saying "you should call him." A referral program makes that instinct tangible — a real thank-you for the people who bring new clients in, without turning the network into a sales team.

The program · proposed

A meaningful thank-you, disclosed openly.

Simple and small on purpose. The goal is to honor the act of sharing, not to build an affiliate funnel.

Who qualifies

Past clients, the network, and trade partners

  • Past clients Tim has delivered a car for
  • Personal network who makes a warm intro
  • Trade partners (mechanics, restoration shops, marque specialists) who send a serious buyer
  • Not: strangers who pass along a link — the program rewards relationships, not traffic
The thank-you

$500 flat per engagement that closes

  • Paid after the new client's engagement wraps
  • Past clients can take cash or a credit against their next engagement
  • Trade partners can take cash or bank it as reciprocal work
  • An annual note of thanks to the year's senders — keep it personal, even when the amount is small
Disclosure

On the table, never hidden

  • Every new client is told at intake who sent them
  • And that the sender will receive a thank-you fee
  • The client's fee is the same either way — Tim pays this out of his side, not theirs
  • Trust stays intact because nothing is buried
The guardrails

Keep it small, keep it honest

  • One step only — a referrer can't earn fees on clients their referrals refer
  • Bad-fit clients don't count. If a trade partner keeps sending mismatches, the thank-yous stop
  • Cap the annual total per referrer if a single person starts driving a large share — a signal to talk, not to scale
  • Non-cash alternatives welcome: a day at a track, a classic dinner, a curated gift — sometimes more memorable

Four calls still open on the program

  1. Flat or percentage. $500 flat is simple and fair on most engagements. 10% of the engagement fee flexes with the work ($250 on a Driver, $450 on an Icon, $750+ on a Collector) — which feels more proportional but more complicated. Pick one; mixing both muddies it.
  2. Warm intros that don't close. Some concierges pay a token "thanks anyway" ($100) for any intro that turns into an intake call, even if it doesn't go further. Recognizes the effort without over-rewarding it. Worth it, or overcomplication?
  3. Announce or not. Two schools. (A) Talk about the program openly on the site and at intake — "reputation is how we grow, and we thank the people who share it." (B) Keep it quiet — pay it when it happens, don't advertise it. Option A invites referrals; Option B preserves the feel of a gift. Tim's instinct probably leans B.
  4. Past clients specifically. Repeat clients are different from first-timers. For them, a credit against their next engagement is almost always more meaningful than $500 cash. Worth codifying: past clients get credit by default, cash on request.

Entity & insurance · staged setup

A founder's sequence. Nothing before it's needed.

The goal is to be protected before money moves and unencumbered before it does. Phases correspond to the year-one roadmap above. Not legal advice — a one-hour paid consult with a CT business attorney is the highest-leverage expenditure in the entire setup.

Phase 01 · Now – first call

Zero-cost prep

  • Draft engagement-letter template (scope, fee, disclaimers, PPI language)
  • Reserve CT LLC name via Secretary of State portal ($60, 120-day hold)
  • Route all concierge-related spend to one dedicated credit card
  • Begin attorney search in the collector network
Phase 02 · Next 30 days

Find two professionals

  • CT business attorney with broker/dealer experience · ~$400 consult
  • Single question to answer: at what volume does CT DMV require motor-vehicle-dealer licensure for a buyer's agent?
  • Small-business insurance broker · request E&O + GL quotes at $1M/$1M and $2M/$2M
  • Keep quotes in a folder, unbound — activate on day of first engagement
Phase 03 · First signed engagement

Formation day

  • File single-member LLC with CT SoS (~$120, online, ~7 days)
  • Get EIN from IRS (free, 10 minutes)
  • Open business checking + card; deposit $500–$1,000 capital
  • Bind E&O + GL policies before taking client deposit
  • Sign engagement letter naming the LLC as counterparty
Phase 04 · Ongoing

Guardrails

  • Never take title — even briefly in transit
  • Never accept a seller-side finder's fee
  • Never co-mingle personal and business funds
  • Verify CT bond/licensure thresholds with attorney by year-end
  • Written coordinator-not-shipper language in all transport agreements

Where things stand · four decided, two to finish

Decisions captured. A few still open.

Six calls that shape the shop

  1. Scope of service · Decided. Buy-side is the practice. Sell-side is available as an add-on — the arc in reverse for clients thinning a collection — but not the headline.
  2. Sub-lane · Deferred by design. Stay broad across 1965–1999 for the first ten engagements. Let reputation — not guesswork — narrow the lane.
  3. Fee model · Decided. Flat tiers for end-to-end plus scoped à la carte. Flat reads as trust; percentage-of-purchase creates a conflict of interest (bigger car, bigger fee, bigger incentive to push).
  4. Transport · Partnered now, own later (maybe). Rent and coordinate through year one. If volume and utilization support it, ownership comes onto the table — a break-even projection will answer the question rather than instinct.
  5. Kendra's role · Silent partner. Design, brand, site, and portal behind the scenes. Tim is the client-facing principal and the voice of the practice.
  6. Entity and insurance · Staged. LLC at the first paid engagement; E&O and general liability before the third; Connecticut-specific broker/dealer licensing verified with a local attorney before year-end.
